# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Motivation

Radar is an important sensing technology used in automotive systems, surveillance, aviation, industrial monitoring, and other applications that require reliable detection and estimation of objects in the surrounding environment. Unlike optical sensing systems, radar operates by transmitting electromagnetic signals and analysing the reflected signals received from objects. This enables radar systems to estimate properties such as target range, radial velocity, and direction of arrival. Radar sensing is also valuable in situations where lighting conditions or environmental factors may limit the effectiveness of optical sensors.

Recent developments in millimetre-wave Frequency-Modulated Continuous-Wave (FMCW) radar have enabled compact sensing systems with increasingly sophisticated perception capabilities. FMCW radar estimates target range by analysing the frequency difference between transmitted and received signals, while Doppler processing provides information about the relative motion of targets. Measurements obtained using multiple receiving antennas can additionally provide spatial information, including the direction of arrival. These processing operations produce structured representations such as Range-Doppler maps and Range-Azimuth-Doppler tensors, which can be used for subsequent detection and perception tasks.

Conventional radar systems rely on established Digital Signal Processing (DSP) techniques to extract useful information from received signals. Operations such as Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT), Moving Target Indication (MTI), Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) detection, angle-of-arrival estimation, and spatial clustering form important stages of a conventional radar processing pipeline. These methods are mathematically interpretable and are based on well-established signal-processing principles. However, their performance can be affected by complex operating conditions involving multiple closely spaced targets, non-uniform clutter, low signal-to-noise ratios, and overlapping target reflections. Their performance may also depend on assumptions about the statistical properties of noise and clutter, as well as the selection of suitable processing parameters.

Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning and deep learning, offers additional approaches for extracting information from radar data. Neural networks can learn representations directly from radar measurements and identify patterns that may be difficult to capture using manually designed detection rules and thresholds. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), for example, can process radar maps as structured spatial representations and learn features useful for target detection and classification. More advanced architectures and learning techniques also provide opportunities to improve detection performance under challenging conditions. Consequently, AI-based methods are increasingly relevant to the development of radar perception systems.

However, the adoption of deep learning introduces an important engineering consideration: computational efficiency. Modern neural networks can require substantial processing operations, memory, and hardware resources, particularly when operating on high-resolution radar tensors or processing successive radar frames at high rates. Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) provide parallel computing capabilities that can accelerate such workloads. Nevertheless, access to dedicated GPU resources may be constrained by hardware availability, acquisition and operating costs, and deployment requirements. In particular, solutions that depend on powerful accelerators may be difficult to reproduce or deploy in resource-constrained environments.

This creates a need to investigate AI-based radar processing not only in terms of detection performance but also in terms of the computational resources required to achieve that performance. A model that provides good detection accuracy but requires substantial computing resources may not be the most practical solution for deployment. Conversely, a highly compact model or a CPU-oriented implementation may reduce hardware requirements but could introduce trade-offs in accuracy, latency, or throughput. Identifying a suitable balance between these factors is therefore an important research problem.

The present project builds upon an existing radar processing and deep learning framework. The current foundation includes a simulated 77 GHz FMCW radar environment, conventional radar DSP modules, a CNN-based radar detection pipeline, and experiments involving the RadDet wideband radar spectrum detection dataset. These components provide a basis for understanding the radar processing workflow, establishing reference implementations, and investigating the computational characteristics of AI-based detection.

Within this foundation, the project adopts a broader research direction centred on **resource-efficient AI for radar signal processing and detection**. Two complementary possibilities will be investigated. The first is to reduce the overall computational requirements of AI-based radar detection while retaining acceptable detection performance. The second is to investigate CPU-oriented models and inference implementations that can operate efficiently without requiring dedicated GPU hardware. Techniques such as lightweight neural architectures, model compression, quantisation, knowledge distillation, pruning, and adaptive computation may be relevant to these investigations.

The objective is not to assume that one particular technique or hardware configuration is necessarily optimal. Instead, the project seeks to establish an experimental basis for comparing alternative approaches and understanding their respective advantages and limitations. The resulting findings will guide the selection of a practical implementation pathway for the subsequent project phase, taking into consideration both computational efficiency and the hardware resources available for demonstration.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

AI-based radar detection offers the possibility of extracting useful target information from complex radar representations. However, the practical adoption of such approaches is influenced by their computational requirements, memory consumption, inference latency, and dependence on specialised hardware.

A radar detection model that achieves high detection performance but requires substantial GPU resources may be difficult to deploy in environments with limited accelerator availability or computational budgets. Similarly, reducing the model size or moving inference to a CPU does not automatically guarantee that the resulting system will be sufficiently accurate or responsive for the intended application. A systematic evaluation is therefore required to understand the relationship between detection performance, computational cost, and hardware requirements.

The central problem addressed in this project is:

**How can AI-based radar signal processing and detection be made computationally efficient and practically deployable while maintaining acceptable detection performance under realistic hardware constraints?**

The investigation will consider two candidate approaches:

1. **Reduction of overall computational requirements:** Investigating techniques that reduce the computation and memory required by AI-based radar detection. Potential methods include lightweight neural network architectures, model pruning, quantisation, knowledge distillation, and adaptive computation. The objective is to reduce the resources required for inference while maintaining an acceptable level of detection performance.
2. **CPU-oriented model development and deployment:** Investigating whether suitable radar detection models can be designed or adapted to operate efficiently on commodity Central Processing Units (CPUs). This includes evaluating CPU-friendly architectures, numerical representations, and inference implementations to reduce dependence on dedicated GPU hardware. Where appropriate, the performance of CPU-based execution will be compared with GPU-accelerated execution under controlled conditions.

These approaches address related but distinct aspects of computational efficiency. Reducing the number of operations or model parameters does not necessarily guarantee efficient execution on every hardware architecture. Likewise, a model that performs efficiently on a CPU may not achieve the lowest overall computational cost. The two approaches will therefore be examined using a common evaluation framework.

The comparison will consider detection performance, computational complexity, model size, memory consumption, inference latency, throughput, and hardware accessibility. Appropriate detection metrics, such as precision, recall, and mean Average Precision (mAP), will be selected according to the detection task and dataset. Computational measurements will include parameters, Floating-Point Operations (FLOPs) or Multiply-Accumulate Operations (MACs), peak memory usage, and inference time. CPU and GPU comparisons will be conducted using documented hardware and software configurations to ensure that the results are reproducible and interpretable.

The final research pathway will be selected after evaluating the feasibility and performance of the candidate approaches. It may involve a computationally reduced model, a CPU-oriented implementation, or a combination of both, depending on the experimental findings and the computational resources available. The intended outcome is a practical and reproducible AI-based radar detection system that provides a meaningful balance between detection effectiveness and computational feasibility.

## 1.3 Objectives

The primary objective of this project is to investigate resource-efficient AI techniques for radar signal processing and detection, building upon an existing FMCW radar simulation, conventional DSP, and deep learning framework.

The specific objectives are as follows:

1. **Establish a radar processing foundation:** Develop and maintain a modular FMCW radar processing pipeline incorporating conventional signal-processing operations such as range and Doppler estimation, clutter suppression, CFAR detection, angle estimation, and target clustering.
2. **Study AI-based radar detection:** Investigate neural-network-based approaches for extracting target information from radar representations and establish suitable reference implementations and detection-performance benchmarks.
3. **Characterise computational requirements:** Analyse the computational and memory requirements of existing AI-based radar detection approaches using metrics such as model parameters, FLOPs or MACs, peak memory consumption, inference latency, and throughput.
4. **Investigate computational reduction techniques:** Explore approaches for reducing the inference cost of radar detection models, including lightweight architectures, quantisation, pruning, knowledge distillation, and adaptive computation, wherever suitable for the selected detection task.
5. **Investigate CPU-oriented inference:** Evaluate the feasibility of executing selected radar detection models on CPU hardware. Study suitable architectural and implementation choices and compare their execution performance with GPU-based inference under controlled experimental conditions.
6. **Establish a comparative evaluation framework:** Compare the candidate approaches using consistent datasets, evaluation procedures, and performance criteria, considering detection quality, computational complexity, memory requirements, inference latency, throughput, and hardware accessibility.
7. **Define a practical demonstration for the subsequent project phase:** Identify the software dependencies, datasets, model requirements, and hardware configurations needed to demonstrate the selected radar detection approach. Document the CPU-only and GPU-accelerated execution possibilities, including their respective computational and memory requirements.
8. **Select a feasible research pathway:** Use the experimental results and available computational resources to determine whether the subsequent implementation should primarily focus on reducing overall computation, enabling efficient CPU-based execution, or combining both approaches.

The work undertaken during the first project phase establishes the radar processing and experimental foundation required for this investigation. The subsequent phase will focus on developing and evaluating the selected resource-efficient approach and demonstrating its practical feasibility.
